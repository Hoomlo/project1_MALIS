\subsection{Formal Algorithmic Rule (1-Sample Explore-Then-Commit)}
For any bay with $K=5$ spots over $T$ days ($T \ge K$), let $A_t \in \{0,...,K-1\}$ be the spot chosen on day $t$ and $R_t$ the observed catch:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Exploration ($1 \le t \le K$):} Sample each spot once without replacement ($A_t = t-1$) and record the initial catch $r_{A_t} = R_t$.
    \item \textbf{Commitment ($K < t \le T$):} Select the empirically best spot $a^* = \arg\max_a r_a$ (breaking ties randomly) and play $A_t = a^*$ for all remaining days.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Task 3: Separating Decisions from Luck \& Season Comparison}
\subsection{Performance Measure Derivation}
Raw total catch $R_{total} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t$ conflates decision quality with two sources of luck in \texttt{fishing.py}: (1) bay richness, since spot means $\mu_t(a)$ are drawn uniformly in $[2.0, 10.0]$ kg, and (2) daily Gaussian noise, since $R_t = \max(0, \mu_t(A_t) + \sigma\epsilon_t)$ with $\sigma = 2.5$ kg.
Using \texttt{bay.best\_possible()} and \texttt{bay.reveal()}, where $\mu_t^* = \max_a \mu_t(a)$, we define two normalized metrics:

\begin{equation}
\rho_{empirical} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mu_t^*}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\eta_{expected} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mu_t(A_t)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mu_t^*} = 1 - \frac{Regret_{expected}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mu_t^*}
\end{equation}

While $\rho_{empirical}$ removes bay richness disparity, $\eta_{expected}$ also eliminates daily Gaussian noise $\sigma$, measuring purely the expected value of the chosen actions. Running \texttt{RandomAgent} across 500 random Season 1 bays confirms that relative dispersion ($CV = SD/Mean$) drops from 19.18\% (raw catch) to 15.96\% ($\rho_{empirical}$) and 14.55\% ($\eta_{expected}$), achieving a 24.1\% variance reduction (Table \ref{tab:variance}).

\section{Task 4: Bandit Algorithms Description}
We implemented three agents in \texttt{agents.py}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HumanRuleAgent (Soren):} It explores sequentially for 5 days, saves the best empirical mean, and commits to it for the remainder of the season.
    \item \textbf{EpsilonGreedyAgent (Thomas):} Follows Section 2.2, selecting a random action with probability $\epsilon$ and exploiting the best known empirical mean with probability $1 - \epsilon$. We introduced a constant step-size parameter $\alpha$ (Section 2.5) to track non-stationary problems (like for season 3).
    \item \textbf{UCBAgent (Soren):} Upper Confidence Bound selection (Section 2.7). It selects actions that maximize $Q(a) + c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N_t}}$, inherently balancing exploitation (high $Q(a)$) and exploration (low $N_t$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Identifying the Best Agents for Seasons 2 and 3}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Season 2:} On Decker's specific 10-day log (Table \ref{tab:agent_comp}), \texttt{EpsilonGreedy} scored an incredible 92.3 kg. However, over 200 random 20-day bays (Table \ref{tab:evaluation}), it averages only 124.8 kg, heavily underperforming \textbf{UCB} (152.3 kg). This exposes a major algorithmic flaw: standard \texttt{EpsilonGreedy} defaults to choosing Spot 0 on day 1. If Spot 0 yields a positive catch, it greedily exploits it for the rest of the short season, exploring other spots only 10\% of the time. Decker's seed was a statistical "miracle" where Spot 0 happened to be optimal. Globally, \textbf{UCB} is the best short-term agent because its mathematical formulation forces an initial exploration of all arms, reliably finding the true optimum and safely beating \texttt{EpsilonGreedy}'s blind bias.
    \item \textbf{Season 3:} \textbf{UCB} is the most robust. On Decker's 30-day shift bay, the \texttt{HumanRule} won purely by luck because its initially chosen spot happened to remain optimal after the shift. When tested properly in a 100-day environment with a shift at day 50, \texttt{UCB} (769.0 kg) vastly outperformed \texttt{EpsilonGreedy} (614.4 kg). At a mere 10\% exploration rate, \texttt{EpsilonGreedy} lacks the momentum to discover the new optimum and override its historic bias quickly enough, even with $\alpha=0.1$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 6: Contextual Bandits}
To leverage the wind, waves, and tide data provided in \texttt{ContextualBay}, we built a \texttt{KNNContextualAgent}. Because distances must not be disproportionately dominated by wind speed, we normalized the context space by the maximums found in \texttt{fishing.py}: \texttt{[40.0, 3.0, 2.0]}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{knn.png}
    \caption{Comparison between pure K-NN, Epsilon Greedy K-NN and UCB algorithms}
    \label{fig:knn}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The Failure of Pure Greedy Prediction:} 
When implementing pure K-NN prediction (Greedy, $\epsilon=0.0$), the agent failed. It scored a mean of 1828.2 kg over 300 days—even worse than a purely Random agent (1945.7 kg). If the agent initially receives a poor reward (due to variance) on a spot under specific weather conditions, the K-NN predicts a low catch for similar future conditions. The agent then avoids that spot entirely, preventing it from ever updating its incorrect prediction.

\textbf{The Fix and Comparison:}
To fix this, we introduced $\epsilon$-greedy exploration ($\epsilon=0.1$) on top of the K-NN. This forces the agent to sample untried spots, gathering robust data across all weather profiles. With this fix, the agent surged to 2664.0 kg, easily outperforming the naive predictor. 
While the \texttt{UCBAgent} scored slightly higher globally (2804.9 kg) due to the "$\epsilon$-tax" (the fixed 10\% exploration penalty the KNN incurs even when fully trained), the \texttt{KNNContextualAgent} proves that incorporating context drastically increases the ceiling of predictive accuracy over long horizons.
